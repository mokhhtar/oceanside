Why Chlorine & Salt Water Turn Hair Green or Brassy (And How to Fix It)

It's Not the Chlorine — It's Copper

Chlorine itself doesn't have a pigment, and it doesn't turn hair green on its own. The actual cause is copper — dissolved in pool water from copper-based algaecides, corroding copper pipes, or the fill water itself. On its own, copper doesn't bind to hair particularly well. But chlorine oxidizes that copper into a form that does bind tightly to the keratin in your hair shaft, depositing a thin metallic film with every swim.

Chlorine still plays a real role in hair damage on its own — it breaks down cysteine bonds and roughens the cuticle, similar to how hard water mineral buildup disrupts the cuticle at home. But the green color specifically is a copper problem, not a chlorine problem, which is why fixing it takes a different approach than a standard clarifying wash.

Why Blonde & Chemically-Treated Hair Show It Most

Everyone who swims regularly in copper-treated water accumulates some metal deposit on their hair. Darker hair usually just doesn't show it, because the underlying pigment masks the green tint.

Blonde, bleached, highlighted, or otherwise chemically-treated hair has two things working against it: it has little to no pigment left to hide the discoloration, and its cuticle is already more porous and damaged, so it absorbs copper faster and holds onto it more stubbornly. This is the same porosity issue that makes bleached hair more vulnerable to mineral buildup from hard water — damaged hair absorbs more of whatever is in the water, for better or worse.

Salt Water's Different Damage: Dehydration, Not Discoloration

Ocean water doesn't contain the chlorine-and-copper combination that causes green tint, so a day at the beach won't turn your hair green. But salt water causes a different kind of damage: it's a strong osmotic dehydrator. Salt pulls moisture out of the hair shaft, leaving the cuticle rough, tangled, and prone to breakage — closer to the dryness pattern you'd see from hard water hair damage than to the metallic staining chlorine causes.

Combine an ocean swim with an Oceanside shower rinse afterward, and hard water minerals can compound the dehydration salt water already started — one more reason a proper post-swim rinse and conditioning routine matters here specifically.

How to Prevent It Before You Swim

  • Saturate hair with clean water first. Hair that's already wet with fresh water absorbs noticeably less pool or salt water, since the shaft is already full.
  • Apply a leave-in conditioner or light oil. This creates a barrier that slows both copper penetration and salt-driven moisture loss.
  • Braid it or tuck it under a cap. Less surface area exposed means less absorption, especially for chemically-treated hair.
  • Rinse immediately after getting out. The first few minutes matter most — copper and salt bond more permanently the longer they sit on the hair shaft.

How to Remove Green or Brassy Tint After It Happens

Once copper has deposited, a regular shampoo won't touch it — surfactants are built to dissolve oil, not break metal-to-protein bonds. You need something that specifically targets the metal:

  • Chelating shampoo: The most reliable fix. These use ingredients like EDTA or citric acid to bind copper ions and lift them away. See our breakdown of metal detox shampoo vs. regular shampoo for how this actually works and whether you need it.
  • Tomato juice or ketchup: There's a real, if weak, mechanism here — mild acidity can help loosen some copper deposits, similar in principle to a vitamin C treatment. It's messy and inconsistent, though, and won't match a proper chelating shampoo.
  • Diluted white or apple cider vinegar rinse: Helps as a maintenance step between washes. For how often you can safely use acidic rinses without over-drying, see our guide on ACV rinses and clarifying shampoo frequency.

Avoid reaching for more bleach or toner to "cancel out" the green — it doesn't remove the copper, and bleaching already-porous hair on top of a metal deposit usually makes the discoloration worse and more uneven.

When Home Fixes Aren't Enough: Professional Color Correction

Heavy or long-standing copper buildup, especially on lightened hair, sometimes needs a professional clarifying treatment or color correction to fully resolve without further damaging the hair. If a chelating shampoo isn't cutting it after a few washes, it's worth booking a consultation rather than repeatedly bleaching or toning at home.
